Thom’s Seven Elementary Catastrophes

How the Robot Co-Design Laboratory uses SVD, higher-order directional analysis, and numerical continuation to screen and verify singular behavior in a design-to-performance map.

Why catastrophe analysis appears in a robot design tool

The co-design problem maps a high-dimensional structural design x into selected performance quantities F(x). The local Jacobian J = ∂F/∂x describes how small design changes affect those performances. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) exposes directions in which that local map becomes weak or loses rank.

Design x → Performance F(x) → Jacobian J → SVD → Critical subspace → Higher-order tests → Continuation / verification

A small singular value is therefore a screening signal, not by itself proof of a catastrophe. The application follows the weak direction into higher-order geometry and, where implemented, performs additional numerical verification.

Corank-one route: fold to butterfly

If one active singular value becomes small while the others remain separated, the application identifies a one-dimensional critical direction. Let v and u denote the associated right and left critical singular vectors. Higher-order derivatives are projected onto that direction.

a₂ = uᵀD²F[v,v],    a₃ = uᵀD³F[v,v,v],    …

The hierarchy is conceptually:

Fold → Cusp → Swallowtail → Butterfly

A generic fold has a rank loss with a nonzero quadratic term. A cusp requires the quadratic fold term to degenerate while the next appropriate term remains nonzero, together with the required unfolding/transversality conditions. Swallowtail and butterfly require successively higher degeneracy and additional independent controls.

Corank-two route: the umbilics

The umbilic catastrophes are different. They require two interacting critical directions. SVD provides a natural screen: if the two smallest active singular values simultaneously approach zero, the critical subspace becomes two-dimensional.

Vc = [vm−1   vm],    Δx = Vc[z₁ z₂]ᵀ

The nonlinear map must then be projected into this two-dimensional critical subspace. Its quadratic/cubic forms and unfolding geometry are used to distinguish possible hyperbolic, elliptic, or parabolic umbilic behavior. This analysis is not yet implemented in the current application.

Evidence ladder

Screened → Candidate → Higher-order verified → Continuation verified → Numerically verified singularity → Thom classification supported

This ladder prevents a numerical optimizer, an active design bound, poor scaling, or an infeasible target boundary from being mistaken for intrinsic catastrophe geometry.

Important mathematical distinction

This application begins with a design-to-performance map, not automatically with a classical equilibrium potential. SVD rank loss and higher-order tests can establish singularities of that map. Describing a result specifically as a Thom catastrophe requires the assumptions appropriate to the mathematical object being classified, including smoothness, the required degeneracy/nondegeneracy conditions, and a sufficiently independent unfolding by the control parameters. Where a potential or equilibrium formulation is required for a particular interpretation, that must also be justified rather than assumed.

Accordingly, the application uses conservative labels such as fold candidate during screening and separates numerical singularity verification from the stronger catastrophe-theory interpretation.

Development roadmap

Current: fold screening and verification; cusp candidate screening. Next: complete fold continuation/bound diagnostics, strengthen cusp verification, then add higher-order corank-one analysis for swallowtail and butterfly. Later: monitor the full active singular spectrum for simultaneous two-direction rank loss and implement the projected two-dimensional analysis needed for the three umbilics.

Fold normal-form verification

The continuation workflow now performs an explicit local normal-form diagnostic at the best rank-loss point. It defines the critical coordinate z = vcT(x−xc) from the weakest right-singular direction and the control displacement μ = control−controlc, then tests whether the shrinking-neighborhood data satisfy μ ≈ C z².

It also fits μ=c₀+c₁z+c₂z²+c₃z³ over progressively smaller neighborhoods and reports quadratic fit quality plus linear/quadratic and cubic/quadratic contribution ratios. Increasing quadratic dominance is numerical evidence for local equivalence to the fold normal form.

A reduced mathematical potential can then be constructed by integrating the scalar reduced equation: if g(z,μ)=μ−Cz², then V(z,μ)=μz−(C/3)z³, up to smooth rescaling and higher-order terms. This is a local mathematical potential, not a claim that the robot's mechanical potential energy causes the fold.

Interpretation: passing this diagnostic strengthens the connection between the engineering design-map singularity and the canonical fold normal form. It does not by itself establish every hypothesis of a classical Thom catastrophe classification.

Critical-point complement-invariance test

The invariance test keeps the same physical critical design and critical direction. It then applies several smooth, invertible local changes of design coordinates. Each coordinate system induces a different legitimate complementary subspace for the Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition.

For every representation the app refits the local relation μ ≈ C z². The numerical value of C is allowed to change under coordinate rescaling. What should persist for a generic fold is a nonzero quadratic term, strong local quadratic fit, suppression of lower/higher-order contamination, and the same physical critical direction.

This replaces the earlier nearby-anchor SVD test. Re-centering a quadratic fold model at regular continuation points was unnecessarily strong: regular points are not themselves folds, so they need not satisfy a fold equation centered at those points.

Scope: persistence across these coordinate/complement choices is numerical evidence that the local fold germ is not an artifact of one chosen representation. A formal Thom classification still requires the smoothness, corank-one, nondegeneracy, and unfolding hypotheses to be stated and justified.

Formal fold-classification hypotheses for this robot design problem

The numerical evidence now supports a coordinate-robust local fold germ. The remaining step is not another numerical search, but an explicit statement of the hypotheses under which the local design problem admits a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction and the standard fold theorem applies.

1. Local residual map

Let x ∈ Rⁿ denote the local structural design coordinates and let μ denote the selected unfolding control, here the local change in H_max. Write the selected performance equations as

R(x, μ) = 0.

The classification is local: all statements concern a neighborhood of the computed critical design (x_c, μ_c), not the entire global design space.

2. Smoothness hypothesis

Require R to be sufficiently smooth in a neighborhood of the critical design (at least through the derivatives used in the fold reduction). This excludes nonsmooth switching of model definitions, discrete topology changes, eigenvalue crossings that destroy differentiability of the selected modal quantities, and activation of hard design bounds at the critical point. The present computation supplies numerical derivative-robustness evidence, but smoothness is a modeling hypothesis that should also be checked from the governing formulas and implementation.

3. Active corank-one hypothesis

Separate ordinary redundancy from the active design-to-performance directions. At the critical point, the active derivative must lose exactly one rank:

D_x R(x_c, μ_c) v_c = 0,    u_cᵀ D_x R(x_c, μ_c) = 0,

with one additional critical direction v_c and all other active singular directions remaining regular. The observed isolated smallest singular value and continuation rank-loss behavior are numerical evidence for this condition.

4. Lyapunov–Schmidt decomposition

Choose a regular complementary subspace and write locally

x = x_c + z v_c + W w.

Because the derivative restricted to the regular complement is nonsingular, the implicit-function theorem gives a smooth local solution w = w(z, μ). Projection onto the left critical direction then produces one scalar reduced equation

g(z, μ) = u_cᵀ R(x_c + z v_c + W w(z, μ), μ) = 0.

The complement-invariance experiment supports the numerical persistence of the resulting fold germ under several legitimate local coordinate/complement choices.

5. Fold nondegeneracy

At the critical point the reduced equation must satisfy

g(0,0)=0,   g_z(0,0)=0,   g_zz(0,0) ≠ 0.

The nonzero projected quadratic coefficient, derivative-robustness test, and shrinking-neighborhood quadratic fits provide numerical evidence for g_zz ≠ 0.

6. Unfolding / transversality

The selected control must move the reduced equation transversely through the singular set:

g_μ(0,0) ≠ 0.

The existing control-transversality diagnostic provides numerical evidence for this condition. It is this condition that makes the control a genuine local unfolding parameter rather than a tangent reparameterization of the singular set.

7. Local normal form

Under the smoothness, corank-one, regular-complement, nondegeneracy, and transversality hypotheses, the reduced equation has the expansion

g(z, μ) = a μ + b z² + higher-order terms,   a ≠ 0, b ≠ 0.

By smooth local changes of state and control coordinates this is equivalent to the canonical fold equation

M ± Z² = 0.

The corresponding reduced mathematical potential may be written, up to sign, scaling, and smooth equivalence, as

V(Z,M) = Z³/3 ± M Z,    ∂V/∂Z = Z² ± M.

This potential is a local mathematical potential for the reduced scalar equation; it is not asserted to be the robot's mechanical potential energy.

8. Current evidentiary status

Numerically supported: isolated active rank loss; nonzero quadratic curvature; control transversality; derivative robustness; two local branches; pseudo-arclength turning point; shrinking-neighborhood convergence to a quadratic normal form; and persistence of that fold germ under legitimate critical-point coordinate/complement changes.

To justify analytically/document explicitly: smoothness of the selected performance map in the critical neighborhood; absence of a modal/eigenvalue nonsmoothness or active hard-bound mechanism at the critical design; the regular-complement hypothesis for the chosen active/redundant decomposition; and the precise identification of the selected control as a smooth unfolding parameter.

Recommended terminology at the present stage: “coordinate-robust, numerically supported local fold singularity with Lyapunov–Schmidt reduced behavior consistent with the canonical fold normal form.” The stronger phrase “Thom fold catastrophe” should be used only after the modeling/smoothness and unfolding hypotheses above are explicitly justified for the local robot design map.

Model smoothness audit: what follows from the implemented equations

This audit separates assumptions that follow analytically from the current model formulation from conditions that still require a local numerical check at the critical design.

Structural coordinates

The design variables are logarithms of the 20 positive element thicknesses. The physical thicknesses are recovered by t_i = exp(x_i). Therefore positivity is built into the coordinate chart and the map from design coordinates to physical sections is smooth for every finite x_i. The fold found here is not created by clipping a thickness at zero.

Rigid-body inertia metrics H_min and H_max

For the catastrophe analysis the worst-case inertia matrix is evaluated at the fixed configuration q = (0,0). Its entries are assembled from exact piecewise mass moments of the positive element areas. Those moments are polynomial/linear functions of the physical section areas and hence smooth functions of the log-thickness coordinates.

H_min and H_max are the ordered eigenvalues of a real symmetric 2×2 inertia matrix. Each eigenvalue is smooth locally provided the two inertia eigenvalues are distinct. The fold computations show a substantial separation between the selected minimum and maximum inertia levels, so an inertia-eigenvalue collision is not indicated in the critical neighborhood.

Flexible frequencies f1 and f2

The finite-element mass and stiffness matrices are assembled from Euler–Bernoulli elements. For each element, area is proportional to thickness and bending inertia is proportional to thickness cubed. Consequently the assembled matrices vary smoothly with positive thickness. The flexible frequencies are obtained from the symmetric generalized eigenproblem K φ = λ M φ and f = sqrt(λ)/(2π).

A selected modal frequency is locally smooth when its generalized eigenvalue is positive and simple. The implementation removes near-zero mechanism/rigid modes and then selects the first two physically meaningful flexible modes. This introduces the principal remaining smoothness condition: near the fold, the selected modes must stay well separated from each other and from the mode-selection threshold so that no sorting/filtering switch occurs.

What is analytically well supported

Within a neighborhood containing only positive finite section dimensions and no eigenvalue collision, the implemented rigid inertia map, FE matrix assembly, exponential section parameterization, and simple-eigenvalue performance quantities are smooth. Thus there is no evident structural obstacle in the equations to applying a local smooth-map / Lyapunov–Schmidt fold analysis.

What remains to check at the computed critical design

The remaining model-specific check is narrow: record several flexible generalized eigenvalues around the critical design and verify that the modes used as f1 and f2 are simple, remain ordered, and remain comfortably separated from the implementation's mode-selection threshold. It is also useful to record the two inertia eigenvalues and their gap explicitly. These are diagnostics of the smoothness assumptions, not additional searches for a fold.

Current assessment: the implemented equations do not appear to obstruct a smooth local fold formulation. The main unresolved smoothness issue is modal simplicity/selection for the two flexible frequencies at and near the critical design.

Local spectral-smoothness audit

The continuation page now evaluates the remaining model-specific smoothness issue directly at the computed fold neighborhood. At the critical point and the nearest well-corrected continuation designs it recomputes the unfiltered generalized FE eigen-spectrum, then reports the first several flexible frequencies, the f1–f2 and f2–f3 gaps, clearance above the implementation's 1 Hz flexible-mode selector, and the two rigid inertia eigenvalues and their gap.

This is not another catastrophe search. Its purpose is to establish that the selected performance quantities are being evaluated on simple, consistently ordered eigenvalue branches, away from the mode-selection threshold. In that regime the smooth matrix dependence described above supports smooth local eigenvalue performance coordinates.
