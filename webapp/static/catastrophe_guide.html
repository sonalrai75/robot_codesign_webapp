Thom’s Seven Elementary Catastrophes

How the Robot Co-Design Laboratory uses SVD, higher-order directional analysis, and numerical continuation to screen and verify singular behavior in a design-to-performance map.

Why catastrophe analysis appears in a robot design tool

The co-design problem maps a high-dimensional structural design x into selected performance quantities F(x). The local Jacobian J = ∂F/∂x describes how small design changes affect those performances. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) exposes directions in which that local map becomes weak or loses rank.

Design x → Performance F(x) → Jacobian J → SVD → Critical subspace → Higher-order tests → Continuation / verification

A small singular value is therefore a screening signal, not by itself proof of a catastrophe. The application follows the weak direction into higher-order geometry and, where implemented, performs additional numerical verification.

Corank-one route: fold to butterfly

If one active singular value becomes small while the others remain separated, the application identifies a one-dimensional critical direction. Let v and u denote the associated right and left critical singular vectors. Higher-order derivatives are projected onto that direction.

a₂ = uᵀD²F[v,v],    a₃ = uᵀD³F[v,v,v],    …

The hierarchy is conceptually:

Fold → Cusp → Swallowtail → Butterfly

A generic fold has a rank loss with a nonzero quadratic term. A cusp requires the quadratic fold term to degenerate while the next appropriate term remains nonzero, together with the required unfolding/transversality conditions. Swallowtail and butterfly require successively higher degeneracy and additional independent controls.

Corank-two route: the umbilics

The umbilic catastrophes are different. They require two interacting critical directions. SVD provides a natural screen: if the two smallest active singular values simultaneously approach zero, the critical subspace becomes two-dimensional.

Vc = [vm−1   vm],    Δx = Vc[z₁ z₂]ᵀ

The nonlinear map must then be projected into this two-dimensional critical subspace. Its quadratic/cubic forms and unfolding geometry are used to distinguish possible hyperbolic, elliptic, or parabolic umbilic behavior. This analysis is not yet implemented in the current application.

Evidence ladder

Screened → Candidate → Higher-order verified → Continuation verified → Numerically verified singularity → Thom classification supported

This ladder prevents a numerical optimizer, an active design bound, poor scaling, or an infeasible target boundary from being mistaken for intrinsic catastrophe geometry.

Important mathematical distinction

This application begins with a design-to-performance map, not automatically with a classical equilibrium potential. SVD rank loss and higher-order tests can establish singularities of that map. Describing a result specifically as a Thom catastrophe requires the assumptions appropriate to the mathematical object being classified, including smoothness, the required degeneracy/nondegeneracy conditions, and a sufficiently independent unfolding by the control parameters. Where a potential or equilibrium formulation is required for a particular interpretation, that must also be justified rather than assumed.

Accordingly, the application uses conservative labels such as fold candidate during screening and separates numerical singularity verification from the stronger catastrophe-theory interpretation.

Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction for this robot design map

Let the selected performance residual be R(x,μ)=F(x,μ)−p*, with design vector x∈Rⁿ and selected performance equations in Rᵐ. Because the design problem is redundant (n>m), the Jacobian has ordinary design-capacity null directions even away from a catastrophe. The fold test therefore concerns loss of rank in the active m-dimensional design-to-performance subspace, not the mere existence of the baseline redundancy null space.

Locally choose active design coordinates y∈Rᵐ spanning the row-space/active SVD directions and complementary redundant coordinates r∈Rⁿ⁻ᵐ. On a fixed local gauge or continuation slice for r, write the reduced residual as G(y,μ)=0. At a candidate fold, DyG has rank m−1, with right and left null vectors v and u satisfying DyG v=0 and uᵀDyG=0.

Decompose y=yc+zv+w, with w in a complementary regular subspace. The regular projected equations can be solved locally for w=w(z,μ) by the implicit-function theorem. Projecting the remaining equation onto u gives the scalar Lyapunov–Schmidt equation g(z,μ)=uᵀG(yc+zv+w(z,μ),μ)=0.

For a generic fold the required local conditions are g(0,0)=0, gz(0,0)=0, gzz(0,0)≠0, and gμ(0,0)≠0. Equivalently, the leading reduced equation is g(z,μ)=aμ+bz²+higher-order terms, with a and b nonzero. Smooth local coordinate changes then reduce it to the canonical fold equation M±Z²=0.

Connection to the implemented diagnostics: isolated active singular-value collapse supplies the one-dimensional critical direction; a₂ tests the quadratic nondegeneracy; control transversality tests the unfolding direction; pseudo-arclength continuation tests the turning geometry; the paired-design calculation tests the two-solution side; and the shrinking-neighborhood normal-form fit tests whether μ becomes quadratically dominated in the critical coordinate.

Remaining formal issue: the active-coordinate/gauge construction must be shown to be smooth and locally invariant enough that the scalar reduction represents the original redundant design problem rather than an artifact of the chosen continuation slice. This is the main mathematical step still needed before using the strongest Thom-classification language. The reduced potential obtained by integrating g is a mathematical potential for the scalar normal form, not mechanical strain or kinetic energy.

Development roadmap

Current: fold screening, higher-order verification, pseudo-arclength continuation, paired-branch visualization, and shrinking-neighborhood normal-form testing; cusp candidate screening. Next: formalize and numerically audit the active-coordinate Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction, complete finite-bound diagnostics, and strengthen cusp verification. Then add higher-order corank-one analysis for swallowtail and butterfly. Later: monitor the full active singular spectrum for simultaneous two-direction rank loss and implement the projected two-dimensional analysis needed for the three umbilics.

Fold normal-form verification

The continuation workflow now performs an explicit local normal-form diagnostic at the best rank-loss point. It defines the critical coordinate z = vcT(x−xc) from the weakest right-singular direction and the control displacement μ = control−controlc, then tests whether the shrinking-neighborhood data satisfy μ ≈ C z².

It also fits μ=c₀+c₁z+c₂z²+c₃z³ over progressively smaller neighborhoods and reports quadratic fit quality plus linear/quadratic and cubic/quadratic contribution ratios. Increasing quadratic dominance is numerical evidence for local equivalence to the fold normal form.

A reduced mathematical potential can then be constructed by integrating the scalar reduced equation: if g(z,μ)=μ−Cz², then V(z,μ)=μz−(C/3)z³, up to smooth rescaling and higher-order terms. This is a local mathematical potential, not a claim that the robot's mechanical potential energy causes the fold.

Interpretation: passing this diagnostic strengthens the connection between the engineering design-map singularity and the canonical fold normal form. It does not by itself establish every hypothesis of a classical Thom catastrophe classification.
